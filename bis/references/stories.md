# Stories & Scenarios — BIS

> Two different things live in this file. Know which one you're writing.

## Real stories (verified, from Shany)

None on file yet. When a real one exists — a real client, a real number, a real outcome, cleared to publish — it goes here with full detail, the same way `blog voice.md`'s override describes. Until then, this section stays empty rather than getting padded with something that looks real but isn't.

## Illustrative scenarios (blog use only, per the 03/09/2026 override)

Fair game for blog posts. Rules:

- **Never a specific named company.** "A logistics business we spoke with" is off-limits — it reads as a real, verifiable claim. "Picture a business that..." / "Say a retailer with three branches..." keeps it honestly hypothetical.
- **Never a specific invented number presented as a result.** "cut downtime by 40%" attached to a fake scenario reads as a fabricated statistic even with the hypothetical framing around it. Keep illustrative scenarios qualitative (what breaks, what it feels like, what changes) rather than quantified.
- **Use them to make a mechanism concrete, not to manufacture social proof.** The job of an illustrative scenario is "here's what this actually looks like day to day," not "here's proof it works."
- **One per post, max** — same rule as the opinions file. Stacking three hypotheticals in one post starts to read as padding.

### Starter scenarios (adapt, don't recite verbatim)

- **The fourth-branch problem.** A business opens a new location and discovers nobody, including their current provider, can say cleanly whether the existing plan covers it, what it'll cost, or who's responsible if it doesn't work day one. Use when writing about scaling connectivity, multi-branch billing, or provider handover pain.
- **The Sunday-night outage.** Connectivity drops outside business hours, right before something time-sensitive (a stocktake, a payroll run, a POS reconciliation). The business owner spends the outage on hold instead of getting an answer. Use when writing about response times, after-hours support, or the cost of a queue-based support model.
- **The forwarded proposal.** A champion inside a business forwards a provider's proposal to a colleague who's never heard of them — and the proposal falls apart because it leans on adjectives instead of numbers. Use when writing about proposal quality, procurement buyers, or "why specificity matters."

## How to use this file

- Real stories only in the top section, only once Shany's supplied them.
- Illustrative scenarios only in blog posts, never in service pages, proposals, or anything a procurement buyer might read as a claim rather than a device.
